# Lessons from `l1108_1367_n014`

Three new entries, each phrased as a mechanical question. All three are about the same
place in the pipeline where this node has now failed twice in production (a
**permission** emitted from *"We're exploring how to let…"*), and none of them is
covered by the existing list: `P2` asks about the **subject** of the main verb, and on
this span the subject test alone would have caught it — yet two runs under two
different prompts still emitted a permission. That is the signal that the subject
question is not the only question worth asking.

---

## L1 — The matrix verb is about a RULE, not about a case

*Taught by `l1108_1367_n014`: "We're **exploring how to** let developers and users
generate erotica and gore … so long as our usage policies are met".*

**Ask:** strip the span's matrix verb and look at what its object is. If the object is
a *rule, policy, permission or arrangement* — "exploring how to **let** X", "working
toward **allowing** X", "considering **whether to require** X", "plan to **permit** X"
— then the span is a statement **about** a rule, and the rule is its grammatical
object, not its content. The status of that object is *proposed / under exploration*,
and the module's job is to record **that status**, never to emit the embedded rule.

**Why the existing list misses it:** the embedded rule ("let developers and users
generate erotica and gore … so long as our usage policies are met") is complete,
fluent, deontically explicit and even carries its own condition. It reads as a
ready-made `permit generate(M) :- usage_policies_met(M)`. `P2` catches it only if you
correctly identify *"exploring"* rather than *"let"* as the main verb — and "let" is
the verb that looks translatable. The mechanical fix is to identify the object of the
matrix verb before identifying the subject of anything.

**The tell:** the module you are about to write would state, on its own, something the
document elsewhere forbids or has not decided. Here `permit generate(M)` would sit two
lines from `#no_erotica_or_gore` and contradict it.

## L2 — A subordinate clause inherits the subject it elides (a sharpening of P2)

*Taught by `l1108_1367_n014`: "— while **drawing a hard line** against potentially
harmful uses like sexual deepfakes and revenge porn."*

**Ask:** for every clause you are about to attach a status to, name its subject out
loud. If the subject is **elided** (a participial or "while"/"in doing so" clause),
write down which subject it elides, and apply `P2` to *that* subject — not to the
clause's apparent force.

**Why it is not already `P2`:** `P2` says "who is the subject of the **main** verb".
A subordinate clause has no subject of its own to test, so the check reads as
inapplicable and the clause gets scored on its own force instead. "Drawing a hard line
against X" in isolation is as strong as prose gets; borrowing "We" from the matrix
makes it a statement of OpenAI's stance, which routes to `ontology`.

**Corollary worth keeping:** when the subordinate clause *does* state something real,
it usually states a **classification** rather than a norm — here, that sexual deepfakes
and revenge porn are *potentially harmful uses*. Record the classification. It is the
class a later norm would quantify over, so if a subsequent ruling decides the clause is
normative after all, the fix is one assertion rather than a re-translation.

## L3 — When the hedge is the main verb, the hedge is the content

*Taught by `l1108_1367_n014`; extends `P7`.*

**Ask:** does the span's hedge sit *around* a norm you are encoding, or *is* it the
span's main verb? `P7` handles the first case (push the defeater into a body). In the
second case there is no body to push it into — so ask instead: **does the module still
say that this is unsettled, after the hedge has nowhere to go?** If the answer is no,
the hedge has evaporated and what you have written is the unhedged content, which is
precisely the over-assertion.

**The mechanical form:** promote the hedge to a predicate over the thing it hedges —
`under_exploration/1`, `proposed/1`, `not_yet_decided/1` — and put the hedge's force
into the **gloss** ("…and which is therefore not in force by virtue of this passage"),
where a reviewer reads it. `P7`'s warning that "an unconditional `oblige` is
byte-identical to one whose default was dropped" has an exact analogue here: a module
that drops a matrix hedge is byte-identical to one translating a span that never had
one.

## Not a new lesson, but confirmed on this clause

* **Zero stage-2 findings carried no information about the decision that mattered.**
  The module has no acts, no bodies, no read-backs and no borrows, so almost every
  deterministic check had nothing to bite on and it passed first try. The one claim
  that could be wrong — that this span asserts nothing — is invisible to stage 2 by
  construction. Worth remembering before reading a clean stage-2 run as reassurance on
  a small module: **the smaller the module, the less a clean stage 2 means.**
