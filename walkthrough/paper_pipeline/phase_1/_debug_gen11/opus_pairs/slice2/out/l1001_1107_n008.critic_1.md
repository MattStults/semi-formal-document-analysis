## Q0 — SHOULD THIS HAVE BEEN TRANSLATED AT ALL?

**Answer: yes, it should have been translated — this states a norm, not a goal — and
`translated` is the right outcome.** I asked the question explicitly and I take the
"should be able to" candidate seriously, because it is real: *be able to* is an ability
modal, and a sentence of the form "the assistant should be able to X" can be read as a
statement about what OpenAI ought to build rather than about what the assistant is
permitted to do. That reading would put it under `00_task.md`'s "states a goal rather
than a condition" trigger.

I reject that reading on three grounds drawn from the text in front of me:

1. **It carries a trigger condition, which is what the abstention trigger is about.** The
   goal-trigger targets sentences with no antecedent — "we want the assistant to be
   maximally helpful". This sentence has three: *for public figures*, *generally public*,
   *unlikely to cause harm through disclosure*. A sentence that says "in situation S, act
   A is available" is a conditional norm whatever modal dresses it, and the node's own
   `kind: conditional` agrees.
2. **Its position is a carve-out from an immediately preceding prohibition.** The printed
   source text is: *must not respond to requests for private or sensitive information …
   Whether information is private or sensitive depends in part on context. For public
   figures, the assistant should be able to …*. Read as a capability aspiration this
   sentence does no work at that position; read as a permission it does the only work a
   third sentence there could do. Nothing else in the document's structure explains why
   it sits after the prohibition.
3. **P2 and N2 both pass.** The subject of the main verb is *the assistant* — the norm
   bearer, not OpenAI, not the document, not a section. There is no matrix verb whose
   object is a rule (contrast N2's *"we're exploring how to let developers…"*, where the
   subject is OpenAI and the embedded rule is the object). Here the modal complex *should
   be able to provide* IS the main verb and its subject is the bearer.

Residual reservation, recorded but not conclusion-changing: `permit` is a slightly firmer
rendering than *should be able to*, which is hedged twice over (`should`, and `be able
to`). There is no weaker deontic pole available — `prefer` is for comparatives and would
be wrong, and abstaining would delete the carve-out entirely, which is the dangerous
direction on a permission carved out of a prohibition. `permit` is the correct choice of
the four. See P7 below for where the hedge went.

## Licence audit

**⭐ The flagged question: are `root_authority` and `privacy_context_dependence`
manufactured citations, and would `assumed` be right?**

**On the licence semantics alone: yes, `textual` here is a manufactured citation, and
`assumed` with an inference naming the `NEEDS` block would be the honest form.** The test
`00_task.md` sets is "the cited clause says this", and it warns (line 26) *"Do not
manufacture a citation to make a fact look textual. Citing a plausible-looking clause for
something the clause does not actually say is the single worst failure available here."*
Applying that test to each:

* **`root_authority/1`** — glossed *textual*, cited to `l1001_1107_n008`. The narrowed span
  says nothing about authority levels. Neither does the **un-narrowed** printed source
  text: the strings "root authority", "#protect_privacy", "authority" and "chain of
  command" appear **nowhere in the SOURCE TEXT block at all**. The gloss's content exists
  only in the `NEEDS` line, which is graph metadata, not document text. So this is the
  strong case: a `textual` licence citing a node whose supplied text does not contain the
  proposition in any form. The honest form is
  `"licence": "assumed", "cites": null, "inference": "the node's NEEDS block hands this
  meaning for a predicate another node owns; the narrowed span does not state it"`.
* **`privacy_context_dependence/2`** — glossed *textual*, cited to `l1001_1107_n008`. Here
  the proposition **is** in the printed block, verbatim, as the middle sentence: *"Whether
  information is private or sensitive depends in part on context"*. But the node narrows
  away from it. So the failure is one notch weaker — it is P6 (content sourced outside the
  narrowing) rather than a citation with no textual support anywhere. Under
  `PROVISIONAL.md`'s logic (ground 2: a fact absent from the span carrying a citation to
  text that does not support it is "the invented entity behind a passed check"), the same
  remedy applies.

Note the direction of harm is mild in both cases: neither predicate appears in any body,
so neither can enter a derivation, and the "weakest licence in the derivation" rule
therefore has nothing to propagate to. The permission's own licence chain is sound —
`permit` is `textual` and every literal in its body traces to the narrowed span, except
`information_about/2`, which is `assumed` with a named inference. The damage is to the
**link record**: a downstream reader comparing glosses to find the provider node is told
"this node says so" when the node does not.

**But I do not file this as a module defect, because the prompt requires the entry and
demonstrates this exact licence.** Contract 2 of `node_worked_example.md` (line 19)
requires a `concepts` entry for every `NEEDS` name "carrying the meaning the node text
hands you", and `10_output_format.md` (the ⭐ at lines 65–66 of the file, *"every
`requires` entry must also have a `concepts` entry saying what you need it to MEAN"*)
makes it mandatory. The worked examples then supply the licence value by demonstration.
Per instrument 2 of this pass, that routes to `## PROMPT FINDINGS` and must **not** be
filed as "the module is clean".

**⭐ The second flagged question: is `privacy_context_dependence`'s arity 2 corroborated by
anything?**

**No. Nothing corroborates it.** The `NEEDS` line gives no arity, the narrowed span never
mentions the concept, and the module never uses the predicate, so no body constrains it
either. The choice is unforced in the strict sense — but it is also **explicitly licensed**:
`node_worked_example.md` contract 2 says *"you choose the arity: `authority_levels_hierarchy`
becomes `authority_levels_hierarchy/2`"*. So arity 2 is an invention the contract asks the
translator to make.

Is 2 the *best* choice? The `NEEDS` gloss — *"whether information is private or sensitive
depends in part on context"* — is genuinely ambiguous between three readings: arity 0 (a
document-level stance: privacy is context-dependent, full stop), arity 1
(`privacy_context_dependence(I)`: whether *this information* is private turns on context),
and arity 2 (information × context, the module's choice). Arity 2 is defensible because
the gloss makes *context* a parameter that varies, and it is the reading that would
actually be usable in a body. I record that it is uncorroborated rather than wrong.

Two things keep it from being conclusion-changing: the predicate is inert (no body, no
derivation), and the module discharged N8 by writing the argument order into the gloss, so
if the provider node defines arity 0 or 1 the mismatch surfaces as a
`requires-unprovided` link failure plus a visible description disagreement rather than a
silent inversion. That is the failure mode the design wants. **Leave as-is.**

**Third, unflagged, and mine: the `root_authority` gloss says more than the node hands it.**
The `NEEDS` line is exactly *"rules in the #protect_privacy section carry root authority"*.
The module's gloss is *"R is governed at the highest authority level the document has: the
rules of the #protect_privacy section, **which no lower level of the chain of command may
relax**"*. The bolded part is not in the `NEEDS` line and not in the source text; it is
knowledge about how the document's authority levels work, imported from elsewhere. The
prompt's instruction is "carrying the meaning the node text hands you", so this exceeds
even the prompt's licence, and it is the kind of embellishment that matters precisely
because the gloss — not the name — is what links this module to its provider node
(`10_output_format.md`: *"the only way another clause's definition can ever be matched to
your need is by comparing what each one SAYS"*). An over-specified gloss can produce a
false mismatch against a provider that defines `root_authority` more narrowly. Inert for
derivation, real for linking. Filed as finding 1 below, non-conclusion-changing.
