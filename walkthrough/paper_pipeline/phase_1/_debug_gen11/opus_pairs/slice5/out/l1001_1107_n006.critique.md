## 1. THE ABSTENTION ANSWER

**No — this clause should have been translated. It states a norm.** Each of `00_task.md`'s four
abstention triggers, by name:

**(a) Is it a section heading?** No. The narrowed text is a full finite sentence with a modal
verb — *"The assistant must not respond to requests for private or sensitive information about
people, even if the information is available somewhere online"*. It is body prose inside
`#protect_privacy`, not the heading of it. (Contrast the worked example's heading node
`l3995_4164_n001`, which is *about* a heading and still translates, to `ontology`.)

**(b) Does it state a goal rather than a condition?** No. It is a conditional prohibition with a
stated scope: the act (`respond`), the trigger (a request whose object is private or sensitive
information about a person), and an explicitly non-excusing circumstance (public online
availability). There is a determinate situation that violates it, which is exactly what a goal
lacks. It is also not a comparative, so `00_task.md` rule 5b (`prefer`, not `forbid`) does not
apply — *"must not"* is the document's strongest register.

**(c) Is it an example?** No. The surrounding printed block contains material that shades toward
illustration (*"For public figures, the assistant should be able to provide information that is
generally public…"*), but (i) the node narrows the span away from it, and (ii) even that sentence
is a norm, not a dialog specimen. Nothing here is a GOOD/BAD response pair or a worked
illustration; the narrowed sentence is the rule itself. This trigger is the one the previous
cohort missed by translating a span headed `**Example**`; it is not the situation here.

**(d) Is its content inexpressible as rules?** No. It expresses cleanly as one `forbid` over
`respond_to(Q)` with a body binding the request to the information it seeks, plus a
rule-set-level ban (`forbid_body`) recording that online availability may not ground a
permission. Nothing in it is a definitional analogy or commentary of the kind that made
`l1799_1974_n009` an abstention.

**Verdict: translate. `outcome: "translated"` is correct.**

## 2. FINDINGS

### F1 — the two `forbid` asserts on `respond_to(Q)` — `no change` (argued, not assumed)

Span text that grounds it: *"The assistant must not respond to requests for private or sensitive
information about people, **even if the information is available somewhere online**"*.

Assert 2's body is assert 1's body plus `publicly_available_online(I)`. It is therefore
**logically subsumed**: every situation matching assert 2 already matches assert 1, and assert 2
derives no atom assert 1 does not. So the question is real.

It is **not** a legitimate distinct positive ground under `00_task.md` rule 4 / failure mode #14.
Rule 4 exists to stop a module concluding something *by negation-as-failure* and then read-backing
a wrong reason; it asks for a distinct assertion where the clause gives a **distinct sufficient
ground**. "Even if available online" is not a second ground for the prohibition — it is the denial
of a candidate exception. Its proper destination is `forbid_body` (review-list **N6**), which this
module already uses.

Nor is it failure mode #5 (hollow stub). #5 is *"one opaque symbol echoing the document's words
while the referenced content is absent"*. Assert 2 contains no opaque symbol; every literal in its
body is declared, glossed, and used, and the content it names is genuinely present.

What it actually is: a **subsumed restatement whose only product is an English read-back**
(*"…forbidden even though the private or sensitive information about a person that it asks for is
available somewhere online"*). That read-back is informative and is the only place in the module
where the concession is rendered in prose for a reviewer. Against that, a reader scanning
`len(asserts) == 2` could mistake it for extra coverage.

I recommend **no change**, on the anti-over-edit standing rule. Deleting it would take
`len(asserts)` 2 → 1, and the removed claim (C3) *is* still carried — by
`forbid_body: {head: permit, banned: publicly_available_online}` — so a deletion would be
defensible; but it is not required by any contract line, it removes a true and cited read-back,
and the redundancy is inert rather than misleading about the rule's content. This is a judgment
call and I am recording both sides rather than editing.

⚠️ Important adjacent check, and the module gets it **right**: review-list P5's warning that *"a
body added to encode 'regardless of context' WEAKENS the rule"* would have bitten hard here if the
translator had written **only** assert 2. It did not — the unconditional prohibition (assert 1)
stands on its own and does not require `publicly_available_online(I)` to fire. That is the
dangerous direction, and it was avoided.

### F2 — `publicly_available_online` in `forbid_body` under head `permit` — `no change`

Span: *"even if the information is available somewhere online"*.

This is the destination **N6** prescribes: a "regardless of / even if" phrase excludes an
exception, and body-absence alone would only stop *this* module conditioning on it while leaving a
later module free to add the exception. `{ "head": "permit", "banned": "publicly_available_online" }`
bans any rule that would derive a permission from the fact that the information can be found
online — which is precisely what the sentence denies. Notation is correct: both slots are bare
functor names, per the worked example's *"Both slots are bare predicate names: never `permit(A)`
… never `permit/1`"* (node_worked_example.md:144).

One scope observation, recorded but not a defect: the ban is unscoped, so it also forbids grounding
*any* permission anywhere in the corpus on `publicly_available_online/1` — including a
public-figure permission of the kind the wider (un-narrowed) span gestures at. The `forbid_body`
schema offers no way to scope a ban to an act class or a clause, so this coarseness is structural,
not a translator choice. See PROMPT FINDINGS.

### F3 — delivery of the PROVIDES name `privacy_protection_rule` — `no change` (N1 run in both directions)

Delivered as `concepts{arity: 0}` plus a **ground, body-less `ontology` atom**
`privacy_protection_rule`.

N1 asks: *will a situation fact ever unify with this atom?* No — and here that is the **correct**
answer, not the failure N1 warns about. N1's failure case is `side_effect_examples(sending_email)`,
a coined constant standing in for a **KIND of situation** that real case facts should have matched.
`privacy_protection_rule` is not a kind of situation: the node's own PROVIDES gloss defines it as
*"the rule that the assistant must not respond to requests for private or sensitive information
about people"* — it names **an object of the document**. N1 reserves ground atoms for exactly that
(*"Reserve ground atoms for facts about the DOCUMENT (`root_authority(section_x)`), where there is
no situation to match"*). A bodied rule would have nothing to bind and an arity-1 form would have
to invent an argument the node does not supply.

Can a consumer use what was delivered? Yes, and the intended consumer is visible in this very node:
the NEEDS name is `root_authority`, glossed as *rules in the #protect_privacy section carry root
authority*. A consumer writes `root_authority(privacy_protection_rule)` — the name occurring as a
**term**, which coexists with the 0-ary atom without an arity clash. So the delivery is usable in
the shape the graph plainly intends.

Nor is it `00_task.md` rule 5 ("an opaque symbol that echoes the document's words"): the rule's
*content* is not hidden inside the token — it is encoded next to it, in the asserts. The token is
an identifier for that rule, which is what a PROVIDES promise is for.

### F4 — the `root_authority` concepts entry, `licence: "textual"`, `cites: "l1001_1107_n006"` — `minor`, declined as a translator defect

What the licence claims: *"the cited clause says this"*. What the entry says: *"R is a rule of the
#protect_privacy section and therefore carries root authority, the document's highest authority
level…"*. **The narrowed source text says none of this** — it contains no mention of authority, of
root authority, or of the `#protect_privacy` section. The claim comes from the node's NEEDS block,
which is a graph-derived header, not the document text; `PROVISIONAL.md` ground 2 describes exactly
this shape as *"a citation to text that does not support it"*, and `00_task.md` calls a dressed-up
`textual` the single worst failure available.

I am nevertheless **declining to call this a translator defect**, because the prompt teaches it
verbatim — see PROMPT FINDINGS. The gloss's own N8 discipline is a positive: it states the argument
order it assumes (*"the argument read here is the RULE that carries the authority, not the section
that confers it"*), which is precisely the disagreement-surfacing move N8 asks for. The one clause
of the gloss that is neither span nor NEEDS text — *"the document's highest authority level"* — is
outside knowledge about the Model Spec's authority ladder; it is harmless here (it can only surface
as a description mismatch against the providing node) and I do not propose changing it.

### F5 — the `closure` declaration — `no change`

`{act_class: "respond_to", closure: "cepa", reason: "the clause bars responding only to requests
for private or sensitive information about people; responding to a request it does not reach is the
assistant's ordinary business, which this clause says nothing against, so silence permits"}`.

Reason is drawn from the clause (the scope limiter *"requests for private or sensitive information
