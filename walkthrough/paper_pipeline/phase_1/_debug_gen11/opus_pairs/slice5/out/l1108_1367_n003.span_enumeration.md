**What I did: I did not encode any part of it.** No predicate, no assert, no
`ontology` entry, no `forbid_body` entry derives from it.

**Why, and the direction of the risk.** P6 governs: content outside the
narrowing is context, not licence. But the direction matters and is worth stating
because it is the uncomfortable one. That sentence is a **permission** carving an
exception out of my prohibition. Therefore:

- **Importing it** would make my module state a permission the node did not give
  me, licensed by a citation (`l1108_1367_n003`) to a claim the node's narrowing
  explicitly removed — and it would **weaken** the prohibition, since content
  that "aids" in the loose sense while being educational would become permitted.
- **Omitting it** leaves my prohibition **broader than the document's
  paragraph**: a purely factual news-reporting discussion that some case-side
  fact classified as `aids` would be forbidden here, where the full paragraph
  permits it.

**Which error does this pipeline prefer?** Omission. `PROVISIONAL.md` ground 3:
*"The errors are not symmetric. A summary that drops a qualifier yields a rule
broader than the document … That is indistinguishable in the output from a real
rule. A module that keeps the qualifier merely under-states what the summary
wanted, and an under-assertion stays visible and citable."* The asymmetry is
about permission-broadening vs. under-assertion, not about the mechanical
direction of the edit, so it transfers: an imported permission is a **licence to
act** that no citable text supports and that nothing downstream can distinguish
from a real one; a missing permission is an **over-restriction** that shows up as
a refusal someone can trace to this module and this clause id. Ground 2 points
the same way — a `textual` fact citing this node for the However sentence is
exactly the "invented entity behind a passed check".

**What I recorded so the omission is VISIBLE rather than silent.** Three things,
two of them machine-readable:

1. **`closure` for `create_content` is `unclear`, not `cepa`.** This is the
   load-bearing one. `cepa` would assert that this clause's silence about

   only field in the schema that can carry the fact of the withheld exception

   silence you just declined to assert — use `unclear`") applied to an exception

2. **`forbid_body` is left EMPTY, deliberately.** N6 would push a "regardless
   of X" span toward banning the exception term from `permit`. The narrowed text
   contains **no** exception-excluding language, and the full paragraph contains
   the *opposite* — a live exception. Writing a `forbid_body` entry here would
   not merely over-reach, it would ban the document's own carve-out. Recording
   the non-entry because an empty list is otherwise indistinguishable from an
   unconsidered one.

## 6. The `praises / endorses / aids` disjunction — ruling BEFORE drafting

Surface pattern: three-way `or` under the negative-scope verb `must not create`.
P4 (disjunction-as-conjunction) and `ds_opus_loop/out/l171_426_n022.lessons.md`
L6 point in opposite directions on this surface, so L6's cheap test decides it.

**L6's test: does satisfying ONE disjunct satisfy the span?**

**Answer: NO.** Content that only `aids` a violent extremist agenda — without
praising or endorsing it — is still content the assistant must not create.
There is no menu here: the assistant does not get to pick one of three ways to
comply. This is a De Morgan `or`: `must not (A or B or C)` = must not A **and**
must not B **and** must not C.

Second, independent check — **P4's literal wording**: *"do several obliges share
one body where the span says 'or'?"* No. There are no obliges; the statuses are
`forbid`, and each of the three carries a **different body**. P4's literal test
also fails to fire. Semantic ruling and literal test agree, which is the
condition L6 records for signing this call.

**Encoding consequence, fixed now:** one assert per disjunct — three `forbid`
assertions on the same act `create_content(C)`, with three different bodies.
Not three obliges on one body (P4's defect); not one assert with an `ontology`
disjunction collapsing the three grounds (legal, but it loses the distinct
positive reason rule 4 requires and gives one read-back for three different
grounds of failure). Deriving `forbid` three times over for content that does
all three is harmless — the status is idempotent — which is exactly why the
same decomposition would be a defect under `oblige` and is not one here.
