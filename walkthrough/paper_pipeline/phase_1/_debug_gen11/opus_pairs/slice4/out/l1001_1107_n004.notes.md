## UNSURE — what I could not settle

1. **Exemplary vs universal force (P7, unencodable).** `oblige refuse(R)` states a
   requirement, but the document states it *by exhibiting one good response*. Nothing in
   the schema distinguishes "the document requires this" from "the document shows this
   being done well". I chose `oblige` over `prefer` because the span's wording is a
   capability/policy statement (*"I can't bypass paywalls"*), not a comparative, and
   because `ESTABLISHES` says "should refuse" — and rule 5b makes `prefer` the wrong home
   for a non-comparative. A reviewer preferring `prefer` here would be making a defensible
   call; the disagreement is real and I could not settle it from the span alone.
2. **`oblige refuse` vs `forbid bypass_paywall`.** The span's GOOD arm can be read as
   *the refusal is required* or as *bypassing the paywall is forbidden, hence the refusal*.
   I encoded only the first, because `ESTABLISHES` demands two propositions and the
   stage-0 count found exactly two norm-bearing finite verbs — adding a third assert on a
   `bypass_paywall` act would have been content beyond both, and the ground of the refusal
   is preserved in assert 1's read-back instead. If a downstream reader wants the
   prohibition on the underlying act, it is the missing piece; I judged adding it to be
   over-assertion, not under-assertion.
3. **`root_authority/1` arity/identity.** Section-argument vs rule-argument, resolved by
   writing my reading into the gloss (N8) rather than by settling it. The provider node
   may disagree; that disagreement should now be visible in the descriptions.

## Where I believe the PROMPT, not the drafter, produced a questionable choice

* **`closure` on an act the node governs only inside one example.** The schema forces a
  closure entry per act class, and the three values are `cepa`/`cnpa`/`unclear`. A single
  worked example genuinely settles the global reading of silence about `refuse` for
  neither pole, so `unclear` is the only honest value — yet the field's own description
  frames `unclear` as "this clause does not settle it", which will read downstream as a
  drafter evasion rather than as the correct answer for an example node. I state here
  that it is deliberate and reasoned (N7), not a shrug.
* **`prefer` has no way to record that a preference is *conditioned on a prior refusal*.**
  Assert 2 approximates "having refused, offer help" by carrying `bypass_request(R)` in
  its body, which is the situation, not the refusal. The vocabulary has no act-sequencing
  relation, so the *"while offering…"* of `ESTABLISHES` — a claim about the shape of one
  response — is representable only as two independent statuses on two acts.

## The fix, and my disposition: **APPLIED, in full, as proposed**

The critic held that my `root_authority` gloss committed to the wrong reading of the single
argument — I had written the argument as the SECTION; it should be the RULE. I checked both
of its grounds against the sources rather than taking them on the critic's say-so, and both
hold:

1. **The NEEDS line's grammar.** *"rules in the #respect_creators section carry root
   authority"* — the subject of *carry* is **rules** (plural, agreeing with *carry*), and
   *in the #respect_creators section* is a locative modifier on that subject. What carries
   the authority is therefore each rule, not the section. My turn-4 reading inverted the
   subject and the locative. This is the stronger of the two grounds, because it is
   internal to the text I was handed.
2. **The corpus's own precedent.** `resolve_runs/graph_v2/node_worked_example.md` lines
   185–201 is the section-authority case, and it goes the critic's way:
   `guideline_authority(R)` with `R` a **rule**, derived through
   `rule_under_heading(R, unprompted_personal_comments_heading)` — the heading appears as
   the *second argument of a separate relation*, never as the argument of the authority
   predicate itself. `root_authority` is the same predicate shape one authority level up,
   so a provider node modelled on that precedent will emit `root_authority(SomeRule)`.

Taken together, my gloss would have declared it reads that argument as a section while the
provider supplies a rule — a total identity mismatch that no deterministic check catches,
which is the exact failure N8 exists to surface. That my turn-4 edit was *itself* the N8
fix makes this worth recording plainly: **the entry fired correctly and I resolved it in
the wrong direction.** The mechanism worked; my reading was the defect. I am not contesting
any part of it, and I adopted the critic's replacement wording verbatim rather than
paraphrasing it, so the two passes' agreed reading is recorded in one form.

Gloss now reads: *"R is a rule stated in the #respect_creators section of the document and
therefore carries root authority, the top level of the document's authority ordering, so
that R is not set aside by an instruction from a lower level; the single argument is read
here as the RULE, not as the section it sits under"*.

This supersedes UNSURE item 3 above: the arity/identity question is now **settled** on the
NEEDS line's own grammar plus corpus precedent, not merely recorded as a disagreement to
be surfaced later.
