## Q1 — THE FRAME. Should this clause have been translated at all?

**Yes — translating it is correct, and abstaining would have been the defect.**

The span is literally a markdown `####` heading, and `00_task.md` does list "it is a section
heading" among the abstention triggers. But the governing contract here is the node contract,
and `node_worked_example.md` settles this case by name in its second worked example
(`l3995_4164_n001`, a heading-authority node): a node stating a **structural fact about the
document** — which authority a section carries — "goes to `ontology` with `asserts` empty",
and *"an empty `asserts` list is not an empty module."* The example is explicit that such a
node "obliges nobody, and it is still a translation, not an abstention." Bad example #4 in the
same file repeats it: *"'It states no obligation' is not on its own grounds to abstain."*
The discriminator is whether the node establishes anything the document says, not what KIND of
passage it is.

Two further reasons the abstention route is not available here:

1. The node's `ESTABLISHES` is not the heading's *sentence* ("Do not contribute to extremist
   agendas…"). It is the **authority attribute**: `{#avoid_extremist_content authority=root}`.
   That attribute is a fact the document states outright, and it is not a heading-as-navigation
   artifact — it is machine-readable metadata carried in the span verbatim.
2. The node's `PROVIDES` names `root_authority`. Other nodes are waiting on this module to make
   that predicate derivable; abstaining strands every one of them. This module is the provider.

The `00_task.md` heading trigger is aimed at headings that carry nothing but a title. This one
carries an authority stamp. Frame is correct.

## Q3 — THE GLOSS AND THE EXTENSION.

The compiled program derives `root_authority/1` over a union of two populations:

- the constant `avoid_extremist_content_heading`, from the ground atom;
- every `R` such that `rule_under_heading(R, avoid_extremist_content_heading)`, from the bodied
  rule.

The gloss reads: *"the argument — **either a rule of the document, or a heading whose attribute
block stamps its section** — carries root-level authority: the highest level of the chain of
command, which no system, developer or user instruction can override."*

**It describes both, explicitly and by name.** The disjunction is not incidental phrasing; it
is doing exactly the work of announcing that this predicate's extension is heterogeneous — a
heading constant sits in it alongside rule variables. This is the failure the question is
hunting (a gloss that says "the rule carries root authority" while the program also puts a
heading constant in the extension), and it is not present. Gloss and derivation match.

One thing I checked and am recording as a deliberate non-finding: the gloss's tail ("the
highest level of the chain of command, which no system, developer or user instruction can
override") is content the *span* does not state — the ranking lives in
`authority_levels_hierarchy`, a NEEDS from another node. But (a) a `concepts` gloss records
what this module **assumes** the term means, which is precisely what the contract asks for,
(b) the module declares that dependency in `requires` rather than smuggling it, and (c) glosses
are prose: nothing here changes what the program derives. Not conclusion-changing.

## Q4 — WHAT A BORROWER GETS.

**Yes, a mismatch would be visible.** Two independent reasons:

1. **Scope.** The gloss is written as a property of the argument — "the argument … carries
   root-level authority" — with no restriction to any section. A borrower who wrote "the rules
   of MY section carry root authority" would be adding a section-membership condition that this
   gloss plainly does not contain. The disagreement surfaces on a straight prose comparison,
   which is the only linking mechanism the corpus has (per `10_output_format.md`: the only way
   another clause's definition can be matched to your need is by comparing what each one says).
   And the placement is right: the section restriction lives in the **rule body**
   (`rule_under_heading(R, avoid_extremist_content_heading)`), not in the concept gloss — which
   is what lets three sections all contribute to one shared `root_authority/1` without any of
   them claiming ownership of the name.

2. **Sort.** The gloss warns outright that a heading can be the argument. A borrower who assumed
   `root_authority/1` ranges over rules only — the natural narrower reading — reads "or a
   heading whose attribute block stamps its section" and disagrees visibly, rather than linking
   and then quietly receiving a heading constant where it expected a rule. This is failure mode
   #9 (same name, different meanings, linking cleanly and being wrong) and the gloss is written
   so it cannot happen silently here.

The provider's gloss does its job. I am not asked to fix the borrowers and have not looked at
them.
