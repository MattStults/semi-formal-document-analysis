## 1. QUESTION ZERO — SHOULD THIS CLAUSE HAVE BEEN TRANSLATED AT ALL?

**Yes. Translating it was correct, and abstaining would have been the error.** I take each of
`00_task.md`'s four abstention triggers in turn against the *narrowed* span:

> "The assistant should not generate erotica, depictions of illegal or non-consensual sexual
> activities, or extreme gore, except in scientific, historical, news, artistic or other contexts
> where sensitive content is appropriate."

- **Is it a section heading?** No. It is a full finite sentence with a subject (*the assistant*),
  a modal (*should not*), and a verb (*generate*). It is not a title, and it is not the
  heading-authority shape of the worked example either (it does not say what authority some
  section's rules carry; that content is off in the borrowed `system_authority` concept, owned by
  another node).
- **Does it state a goal rather than a condition?** No. This is the trigger worth the most care,
  because "should" can mark aspiration. It does not here. The sentence names a *bearer* (the
  assistant), an *act* (generate), a *class of objects* (erotica / illegal or non-consensual sexual
  depictions / extreme gore), and an *exception* (certain contexts). That is exactly a condition: a
  situation can be checked against it and can violate it. Compare a genuine goal — "we aim to
  handle sensitive content responsibly" — which no situation violates. Rule 5b of `00_task.md`
  routes non-violable comparatives to `prefer`; this one is violable, so it is a norm, and `forbid`
  is right. (Reading "should not" as merely aspirational, and downgrading to `prefer` or
  abstaining, is precisely the weakening the dispatch's TRAP warns about; I decline it.)
- **Is it an example?** No. There is no good/bad response pair, no dialogue, no "**Example**:"
  heading, no illustrative instance. The parenthetical *(e.g., erotic or violent visceral noises)*
  in the surrounding line is inside the sentence the node explicitly **narrowed away**, and even
  there it is an illustration inside a rule, not an example passage.
- **Is its content inexpressible as rules?** No. It is the most expressible shape the corpus has: a
  prohibition over an indexed act, with a disjunctive trigger and a contextual exception. It
  compiles to four `forbid` entries and six ontology entries with no invented normative force.

So the module's `outcome: "translated"` is right, and `abstain_reason: null` is right. I record
this answer explicitly because a silent one counts as unasked.

## 2. THE CENTRAL QUESTION — NEGATION AS FAILURE

The four bodies are `<kind>(C), not generated_in_exception_context(C)`.

### (a) Which direction does the error run, and is it the safe one?

**It runs in the SAFE direction, and it is the safe direction *here* specifically because the
NAF literal guards an exception to a prohibition rather than a ground for a permission.**

Under NAF, `generated_in_exception_context(C)` must be *affirmatively established* for the
exception to bite. An under-specified case — one where nobody said what context the generation
happens in — derives nothing for the exception, so the guard is satisfied and **the prohibition
fires**. The default is *forbidden*. Silence is resolved against the generation of erotica, illegal
or non-consensual sexual depictions, and extreme gore.

The alternative encoding — a positive `non_exception_context(X)` or
`not_generated_in_exception_context(C)` input that the case side must assert — inverts this. Under
that encoding, a case that simply fails to say the context is non-scientific derives no such fact,
the body fails, **the prohibition does not fire, and the module silently permits erotica**. That is
the dangerous direction, and it is dangerous in exactly the way the dispatch's TRAP describes: not
by widening the exception's *text*, but by making the exception the *default*.

So the design here is: prohibition is the default, exception is the burden. That matches the span,
which states the prohibition flatly and marks the exception with "except in" — the syntax of a
carve-out that must be shown, not presumed.

### (b) Do `10_output_format.md` and `00_task.md` license, forbid, or say nothing about `not`?

They **discourage it, in a form that is scoped by the word "only" — and that scope is the whole
question, because this module falls outside it.** The evidence, quoted:

`prompt/00_task.md`, rule 4:

> 4. **Give each way of failing its own positive reason.** Do not conclude something "because the
>    exception does not reach this case" using only negation-as-failure — `not p` carries no account
>    of *why*, and the read-back then states a wrong reason for a right verdict. Where the clause
>    gives distinct grounds, write a distinct assertion on the positive ground.

`prompt/30_failure_modes.md`, failure mode 4:

> | **4** | **Right answer, wrong stated reason** | *misleading.* "Forbidden, because the exception
> does not reach this" — with no account of why it does not reach. Caused by leaning on
> negation-as-failure. |

`prompt/20_worked_example.md`, bad example **3 — reasons from an absence**:

> ```json
> { "status": "forbid", "act": "produce(M)", "body": "not permitted(M)",
>   "read_back": "producing % is forbidden because the exception does not reach it" }
> ```
> `not permitted` carries no account of *why*. The verdict is right and the stated reason is wrong,
> and the read-back asserts a reason the program cannot support. Give the positive ground instead.

`prompt/10_output_format.md` says **nothing** about `not` anywhere — I searched it; the word does
not appear as a body operator in any rule it states.

`schema.py` **permits** `not` structurally and, unusually, discusses it twice as a live and legal
construct rather than a banned one:

> a head variable that appears anywhere in the body may still be unsafe for clingo (e.g. bound only
> inside `not ...`) (`_check_head_bound` docstring)

> A `_` inside a `not` literal is NOT renamed — gringo projects it, so `not r(X,_)` solves where
> `not r(X,_V2)` is unsafe.

So the schema treats `not` as ordinary ASP it will accept, and the prose treats it as a hazard.

**Now: does this module commit the thing rule 4 bans?** No, and the discriminator is not stylistic.
Rule 4's prohibition is on concluding "using **only** negation-as-failure". Bad example 3 is the
degenerate case: the body is `not permitted(M)` and *nothing else* — the NAF literal is the sole
ground, so the rule has no positive reason at all, and its read-back ("because the exception does
not reach it") asserts a reason the program cannot support. This module is the opposite shape on
both counts:

1. **Every body carries a positive ground first** — `erotica(C)`, `illegal_sexual_activity_depiction(C)`,
   `nonconsensual_sexual_activity_depiction(C)`, `extreme_gore(C)`. The NAF literal is a *guard*,
   never the reason. Rule 4's own remedy sentence — "Where the clause gives distinct grounds, write
   a distinct assertion on the positive ground" — is satisfied literally: four distinct grounds,
   four distinct assertions.
2. **The read-backs do not overclaim.** They say "...and no scientific, historical, news, artistic
   or other context making sensitive content appropriate **has been established** for it". "Has
   been established" is the honest verbalisation of NAF — it reports the epistemic state the
   program is actually in, rather than asserting the metaphysical claim "this is not such a
   context". That is precisely the wrong-stated-reason defect being avoided on purpose. Had the
   read-back said "because it is not an artistic context", I would be filing a CONCLUSION-CHANGING
   finding.

**Verdict on (b): not a defect in the module.** But it is not a clean pass for the *prompt* either
— the contract never states what to do when NAF guards an exception behind a positive ground, and a
mechanical reader of rule 4 or of review-list N5 will "fix" this module into the dangerous
direction. That goes under PROMPT FINDINGS below.

### (c) Exception WITHDRAWS a prohibition ≠ permission on the excepted branch. Right or wrong?

**Right, and this is the module's best work.** Three independent things had to line up and all
three do:

- `asserts` contains **no `permit` entry**. Nothing says generating erotica in an artistic context
  is allowed. The exception only removes the forbid; the branch is left empty.
- `closure` for `generate` is **`unclear`**, with the reason spelling out the distinction —
  *"withdrawing a prohibition is not granting a permission, so what the clause leaves silent about
  generating ... it does not settle"*. Had this been `cepa`, the module would have re-asserted, via
  the closure, exactly the permission it correctly declined to assert directly. Review-list N7 names
  this trap verbatim: *"Same reasoning governs `closure`: a `cepa` closure re-asserts the silence
  you just declined to assert — use `unclear`."* The module already does what N7 asks.
- `claims` C7 states the point in words — *"the exception withdraws the prohibition on the excepted
  branch; it does not itself state that generating such content there is permitted, so this clause
  leaves the excepted branch unsettled"* — so the commitment is inspectable rather than inferable
  from an absence.

Note also that `unclear` covers a second silence correctly: content outside the three named kinds.
The clause says nothing about generating anything else, and `unclear` does not pretend it does.

### (d) Is `sensitive_content_appropriate_in/1` a real duplication of the borrowed `sensitive_content`, and does it matter?

**Not a duplication — the two range over different sorts — and it does not matter for the
conclusions. There is a separate, milder criticism that does land, and it is COSMETIC.**

The NEEDS gloss the node handed the drafter is:

> `sensitive_content`: Content such as erotica or gore that may only be generated under specific
> circumstances (e.g., educational, medical, or historical contexts, or transformations of
> user-provided sensitive content).

That predicate is true of **content** — it classifies a piece of material as sensitive. The coined
`sensitive_content_appropriate_in/1` is true of a **setting** — it classifies a context as one where
such material is fitting. They are not the same relation and could not be substituted for one
another: `sensitive_content(C)` in the `exception_context(X)` rule would be a sort error, since `X`
there is a context, not content. So the module has not shadowed a borrowed name with a private
copy, which is what would make this a real duplication.

Could the module have *used* the borrowed name? Only by writing something like
`exception_context(X) :- generation_context(C, X), sensitive_content(C), ...`, and that would be an
invention: the span's exception turns on the **context**, not on a prior finding that the content is
sensitive, and adding `sensitive_content(C)` as a conjunct to the exception would in fact *narrow*
the exception on a ground the span does not state. Leaving the borrowed name unused is not only
acceptable, it is explicitly contract-required: `node_worked_example.md` says, in bold, *"The NEEDS
name went to `requires` even though the module never uses it. ... Contract 2 is not conditional on
your judgment of relevance."* Review-list P9, in its **corrected** form, says the same: *"A NEEDS
name in `requires` and unused is CONTRACT-REQUIRED and must be left alone."* I leave both
`sensitive_content/1` and `system_authority/1` alone.

The criticism that *does* land: `sensitive_content_appropriate_in/1` is glossed *"X is a setting in
which sensitive material of this kind is a fitting thing to produce"*, which is close to restating
its own name (appropriate → fitting). That brushes failure mode 5 (hollow stub) and review-list P8.
I judge it **COSMETIC and not fixable**: the span's residual category *"or other contexts where
sensitive content is appropriate"* is genuinely open-ended — the document supplies no criterion for
it — so any richer gloss would be invented content, and `00_task.md` rule 5's remedy ("if the
content is not in this clause, it belongs in `requires`") is unavailable because the NEEDS block
does not name it and `node_worked_example.md` forbids inventing a `requires` entry from "no
provider turned up". An honest open placeholder in `inputs` is the least-bad option available under
the contract. See CLASSES RAISED for the mechanical question this generalises to.

### F1 — Four `forbid` entries on one act with four bodies. **CORRECT. No change.**
The span's trigger is a **disjunction**: erotica *or* illegal sexual depictions *or* non-consensual
sexual depictions *or* extreme gore. Each independently suffices. Four bodies sharing one act is the
correct ASP for a disjunctive trigger, and it is the shape `node_worked_example.md` teaches —
*"Alternatives are written by repeating the atom ... never `;` between literals (which clingo reads
as AND)"*. Nothing has been faked in either direction:
- **No disjunction split that should have been one rule.** Collapsing to a single
  `prohibited_kind(C)` intermediate would satisfy the solver but would violate `00_task.md` rule 3

  content to be both erotica and gore before the prohibition fires. Review-list P4 (disjunction

  illegal **or** non-consensual sexual activities" is one noun phrase with a disjoined modifier;
  either property alone triggers. Encoding it as one predicate would make the two grounds
  indistinguishable in the read-back for no gain.

### F10 — Ambiguity I resolved in the module's favour: what does "where sensitive content is appropriate" attach to?
Two readings exist. (i) The relative clause modifies **"other contexts"** only, so the four named
contexts each except on their own — the module's reading, encoded as five independent
`exception_context` rules. (ii) It modifies **the whole list**, so a scientific context excepts only
if sensitive content is also appropriate there — a narrower exception, hence a stronger prohibition.
I adopt (i): it is the natural English parse (the relative clause sits adjacent to "other contexts",
which is the residual catch-all the list is building toward), and it is the reading `ESTABLISHES`
reproduces verbatim without disambiguating. Editing toward (ii) would add a conjunct to four rules
on my own authority and would be an invention, not a repair — review-list N4's warning about
propagating a parenthetical "to be safe" applies. **Recorded as unsettled-but-resolved rather than
silently passed**, because it is the one place a real conclusion could differ. See CLASSES RAISED C5.

## 4. PROMPT FINDINGS

**PF1 — `00_task.md` rule 4 and `30_failure_modes.md` #4 do not state their own scope, and a
mechanical reader will apply them in the harmful direction.**
File: `prompt/00_task.md`, rule 4. Also `prompt/30_failure_modes.md`, row 4; illustrated by
`prompt/20_worked_example.md`, bad example 3.
Rule 4 bans concluding *"using **only** negation-as-failure"*, and its illustration
(`body: "not permitted(M)"`) is the degenerate all-NAF case. But the rule never says what to do with
the far more common shape this clause has — **a positive ground plus a NAF guard on an exception** —
and it never mentions that the safety of NAF depends on which side of the deontic polarity the
negated literal sits on. As written, a critic or a repair loop applying rule 4 mechanically will
rewrite `erotica(C), not generated_in_exception_context(C)` into a positive-only body and thereby
move the default from *prohibited* to *permitted*. On a clause forbidding erotica, non-consensual
depictions and extreme gore, that is a live harm. The module is clean; the prompt is what leaves
this decidable only by a critic willing to reason about polarity from scratch.
**What rule 4 needs:** the scope condition already implicit in its own "only" — *NAF as the sole
ground is banned; NAF as a guard behind a positive ground is required wherever the negated literal
guards an exception to a prohibition, because the alternative makes silence permissive.*
`10_output_format.md` should also say something about `not` at all; at present it is silent, so the
only guidance lives in a rule and a bad example that both describe the degenerate case.

**PF2 — the contract gives no home for an open residual category, and forces a near-tautologous gloss.**
Files: `prompt/00_task.md` rule 5 ("If the content is not in this clause, it belongs in `requires`")
against `resolve_runs/graph_v2/node_worked_example.md` ("This is not a licence to move a borrowed
name into `inputs` ... The discriminator is the `NEEDS` block, never 'no provider turned up'").
The span's *"or other contexts where sensitive content is appropriate"* is an open category the
document does not define and the NEEDS block does not name. Rule 5 sends undefined content to
`requires`; the node contract forbids inventing a `requires` entry not in NEEDS. The only remaining
slot is `inputs`, where the predicate is necessarily an opaque symbol echoing the span's words —
which is failure mode 5 by construction. The drafter took the only route available and I am not
filing it against the module, but the contract currently makes a mild hollow-stub unavoidable on
every clause with an open "or other X" residual, and `00_task.md` should say which way to jump.

## 6. CLASSES RAISED

Each is a question a later reader can apply mechanically to a different clause.

**C1 — NAF polarity.** *For every `not L` in a body: does the enclosing rule's head attach a
restrictive status (`forbid`/`oblige`) or a permissive one (`permit`)? If permissive, unestablished
`L` licenses the act and the NAF is dangerous; if restrictive and `L` names an exception,
unestablished `L` makes the rule fire and the NAF is safe. Flag only the permissive case.*
**MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — yes, fully.** Parse each `asserts[].body` for `not <name>`, read
`asserts[].status`, and emit only where status is `permit`. ~10 lines. This is the correction
review-list N5 needs; N5 as written flags both cases and its remedy is harmful on the safe one.

**C2 — NAF read-back honesty.** *For every body containing `not L`, does the `read_back` verbalise
the negated literal epistemically ("has not been established", "no ... has been shown") rather than
ontically ("is not a ...")? An ontic read-back asserts a reason the program cannot support.*
**MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — partly.** A keyword screen for established/shown/demonstrated/asserted
versus a bare "is not" gets most of it, but the final call needs a reader. Good as a flag-for-review
rather than a pass/fail. This is the discriminator that separates this module from
`20_worked_example.md`'s bad example 3, and it is currently checked by nobody.

**C3 — withdrawal is not permission.** *For every clause whose span contains "except"/"unless"/
"other than", does the module contain a `permit` assert, or a `cepa` closure, on the excepted
branch? Either one converts a withdrawn prohibition into a granted permission the span never
states.*
**MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — yes.** Regex the span for the exception markers, then check
`any(a.status == "permit")` and `closure[].closure == "cepa"`. Generalises review-list N7 from
"unless" to every exception marker and makes the closure half of it mechanical.

**C4 — same-act multi-body integrity.** *Where N asserts share one act and one status, do their
bodies differ in their FIRST positive literal (a genuine disjunction of grounds), or do they differ
