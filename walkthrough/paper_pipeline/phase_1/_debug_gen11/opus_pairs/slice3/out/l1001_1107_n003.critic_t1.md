   markers carry the "should"), and it drops no qualifier the span carries ("full lyrics",

**Cost of abstaining, stated plainly:** abstaining would delete from the corpus the only

## 5. CLASSES RAISED

Each is a question a later reader can apply mechanically to a different clause.

**C1 — Example-heading abstention audit.** For every module whose span text contains a line
beginning `**Example**` (or a `<comparison>` block), does the drafting transcript contain the
string "abstain" at least once, AND does the module's Question Zero get answered in words
somewhere? *MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — yes, trivially: grep the span for `**Example**`/
`<comparison>`, grep the transcript for `abstain`. It cannot check whether the answer was
**good**, only whether the question was **asked**, which is the measured failure.*

**C2 — GOOD/BAD pole discrimination.** For every module whose span contains both a `<!-- GOOD -->`
and a `<!-- BAD -->` marker, do the resulting `asserts` entries differ in `status` **or** in act
functor? A span with two poles compiling to one indistinguishable rule is the defect. *MECHANICALLY
CHECKABLE — yes: detect the two markers in the span, then assert that the module's `asserts` list
contains at least two entries that are not identical on `(status, functor-of-act)`. A module with
only ONE assert against a two-pole span should also be flagged for human look, since it may have
collapsed the poles by dropping one.*

**C3 — Claims-to-asserts residue.** Does every entry in `claims` have at least one predicate or
act functor that appears in `asserts`, `ontology`, or `defines`? A claim whose content appears
nowhere in the encoded artefacts is the fingerprint of a dropped obligation. *PARTIALLY
MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — a keyword-overlap heuristic (stem the claim text, intersect with the
functor and gloss vocabulary of the encoded entries) flags candidates cheaply and with false
positives; deciding whether a flagged claim is genuinely unencoded needs a reader. Worth running
as a triage filter, not as a gate.*

**C4 — Silence-licenses-the-act direction check on negative-qualifier inputs.** For every input
predicate whose NAME is negative in form (`not_*`, `non_*`, `un*`, `without_*`) and which appears
in the body of a `forbid`: note that absence of that input makes the prohibition NOT fire, so
under a `cepa` closure the unknown case is permitted. Is that the direction the span wants, and
does the `closure.reason` acknowledge it? *MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — the detection half is a few
lines: regex the negative name prefixes over `inputs`, check membership in any `forbid` body,
check the act class's closure value is `cepa`. The judgement half (is that direction acceptable
here?) is not mechanical, but the check turns an invisible property into a listed one, which is
the whole value.*

**C5 — Coined-name substring trace.** For every name appearing in `ontology`, `inputs`, or `acts`
that is not a `NEEDS` name, does at least one of its underscore-separated tokens (minus
stopwords) occur as a substring of the narrowed span text? *MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — yes, and it
is cheap. It will not catch a fluent-but-unanchored constant whose tokens happen to appear, but
it does catch the `tiananmen_example` shape, where no token occurs at all.*

**C6 — Unused NEEDS with authority content.** Does the module have a `requires` entry that (a)
is a `NEEDS` name, (b) appears in no body, and (c) whose gloss mentions authority, priority,
precedence, or override — while the module emits at least one `asserts` entry? If so, the
clause's force tier reaches nothing in the compiled program. *MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — yes:
set-difference `requires` against body-occurring predicates, then keyword-match the gloss against
{authority, precedence, override, outrank, priority, tier}, then check `len(asserts) > 0`. This
is a PROMPT/SCHEMA finding generator, not a module defect — flagged modules must NOT be edited
(see PF1); the output is a list for the schema owner.*

**C7 — Prohibition-body context creep.** For any `forbid` whose body contains a predicate
describing what the USER did (a request, a message, an ask) rather than what the assistant would
produce: does the span actually condition the prohibition on being asked, or is the prohibition
unconditional? Adding the request fact narrows the prohibition to the asked case. *PARTIALLY
MECHANICALLY CHECKABLE — the detection is a name heuristic (`requests_*`, `asks_*`, `*_message`)
plus "is it in a forbid body"; the resolution needs the span. Sibling of P5's "a body added to
encode context WEAKENS the rule".*

## 6. WHAT I COULD NOT SETTLE

1. **Whether `forbid` or `prefer` is the document's true force for a `<!-- BAD -->` marking, as a
   corpus-wide convention.** I resolved it for this clause (F6: `forbid`, and I refuse to weaken
   it), and I am confident that is right *here* given `ESTABLISHES` says "should decline" about
   a reproduction the document marks BAD. But the general question — does every `<!-- BAD -->`
   marker in this document carry prohibitive force, or do some mark merely dispreferred
   responses? — cannot be settled from one span, and the answer changes several modules at once.
   The worked-example file's own example (`l4251_4571_n029`, a birthday/voice case) uses
   `prefer` for its GOOD arm and asserts nothing for its BAD arm, which is a *third* pattern
   again. **This is a convention question for the owner, and it should be ruled on in the repo
   rather than decided independently by five critics.** I note that the safe default under the
   measured trap is to prefer the stronger reading, since the harm from weakening has been
   measured twice and the harm from over-strengthening has not.

2. **Whether `closure: cepa` on `provide_full_lyrics` is the right reading for *partial* lyrics.**
   The span's qualifier is "**full** lyrics", and `cepa` therefore permits reproducing a
   substantial-but-not-complete portion of a protected song. I judged `cepa` defensible (F5)
   because the alternative invents a restriction the span does not state, and because the GOOD
   response's offer of an *analysis* implies some quotation is contemplated. But I cannot rule
   out that the surrounding `#respect_creators` prose — which I was correctly not shown —
   settles this, in which case `unclear` would be the more honest value. **Left as-is; flagged
   for whoever holds the whole section.**

3. **Whether `licence: textual` on the `root_authority` concept gloss is an over-claim.** See
   F9. It is inconsequential in the compiled program (no consumers), so I could not justify an
   edit, but I could not fully convince myself it is right either. The general question — how
   much a borrowed-name gloss may elaborate beyond the `NEEDS` line before its licence should
   drop from `textual` to `assumed` — is not answered anywhere in the contract I was given, and
   it will recur on every node with a `NEEDS` block.
