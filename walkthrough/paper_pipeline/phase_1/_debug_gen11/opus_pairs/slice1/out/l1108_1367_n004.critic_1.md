## ⭐ Q1 — THE FRAME: should this clause have been translated at all?

**Yes — this states a norm, and the norm is a PERMISSION.**

The narrowed span is:

> "However, **it may provide** critical, discouraging, or factual discussions (from an
> objective point of view) of such topics in contexts like news reporting, historical
> analysis, or education."

The words that state the norm are **"it may provide"**. `it` is the assistant (P2's subject
test passes: the bearer of the main verb is the model, not OpenAI, not the document, not a
section). `may` is a deontic modal attaching **permit** to the act of providing a
discussion. There is no matrix verb wrapping it (N2 test: the main verb IS `may provide`;
this span is not *about* a rule, it *is* one).

Checking the abstention grounds one at a time, since the measured failure is that this
question goes silently unasked:

* **Section heading?** No — it is a finite sentence with a modal.
* **States a goal rather than a condition?** No — it is fully conditional: the permission
  fires only for discussions of a particular character (critical / discouraging / factual),
  under a stated qualifier (objective point of view), in a stated class of settings.
* **Is it an example?** No. This is the trap the previous cohort fell into, so I checked it
  directly: the span contains no good/bad response pair, no dialog, no `**Example**:`
  heading, and no illustrative transcript. The phrase "contexts like news reporting,
  historical analysis, or education" gives *examples of a context class inside a rule* —
  that makes the class open-ended, it does not make the span an example. The document is
  stating when the permission holds, not illustrating a rule stated elsewhere.
* **Not expressible as rules?** It is expressible, and the module expresses it.

Abstention would have been **wrong** here: it would have deleted a live carve-out from a
root-authority prohibition, which is the highest-cost direction to be wrong in on this
clause. `outcome: "translated"` is correct.

text is silent.

The tell that this is a slip rather than a considered call is **inside the module**: the other
NEEDS name, `root_authority`, is marked `assumed` with an explicit inference saying the
narrowed span says nothing about it. Two borrowed names, same evidential situation, two
different licences.

Graded MINOR, not conclusion-changing: no verdict on any situation changes, because the fix is
`licence: "assumed"` plus an `inference` sentence — the predicate, its arity, its `requires`
placement and every body that uses it stay exactly as they are. But see PROMPT FINDING below;

## PROMPT FINDING

**F1's true cause is at least partly the production prompt, and this module must not be filed
as clean.**

`node_worked_example.md`, contract 2 (lines 19–21), tells the translator to give every `NEEDS`
name *"a `concepts` entry carrying the meaning the node text hands you"* — and then the good
worked example demonstrates that entry as **`textual`, citing the node**:

> `{ "name": "authority_levels_hierarchy", "arity": 2,`
> `  "gloss": "which of two levels of the chain of command is the higher one",`
> `  "licence": "textual", "cites": "l527_796_n012", "inference": null, "toggleable": false },`

`authority_levels_hierarchy` is a NEEDS name — a concept another node owns, whose content the
citing node does not state — and the example licenses its gloss `textual` against the citing
node anyway. The third worked example repeats the pattern with `voice_turn_taking_rule`.

This collides head-on with `10_output_format.md` lines 66–78, which say a `requires` gloss is
*"recording what this clause has to assume about it"* and *"⚠️ **You are not defining the
term.**"* — an assumption about another clause's term is, by construction, not something the
citing clause's text says.

So the prompt teaches, by worked example, the exact licence the module used, while its prose
describes a fact that cannot honestly be `textual`. A translator following the example lands on
F1 every time a NEEDS gloss needs any content beyond the bare name. The module's inconsistency
(`root_authority` `assumed`, `objective_point_of_view` `textual`) is the visible edge of the
prompt's own inconsistency.

Two separate verdicts, not merged: **the module has a real MINOR defect (F1)**, and **the prompt
has a defect that produces it**. The prompt-side repair is to say once, in
`node_worked_example.md`'s contract 2, which licence a NEEDS `concepts` entry carries and why —
and, if the answer is `textual`, to change the worked examples' glosses so they carry only the
words the node hands over.

Secondary, smaller: F3 has a prompt/graph component. Rule 8b requires a superiority claim to go
in `beats` with this clause as sayer, but a node whose sibling half-sentence is the loser is
never given the loser's id, so a genuine "However" carve-out has no field it can land in. That
is a contract gap, not a translator error.

## TRAP CHECK

The brief names the trap: an entry of the shape *"is every entry in `claims` actually encoded —
and can the rule that encodes it ever FIRE?"*, whose analogues are P3 and N1, and whose failure
mode is making a rule fire by weakening what it asserts. I ran both, and I record the result
explicitly.

**Did I charge under P3 or N1?** No.

**Firing analysis, recorded.** I traced every body predicate in the three asserts to a source
that can supply it:

| predicate | where it comes from | can it be supplied? |
|---|---|---|
| `critical_discussion/1`, `discouraging_discussion/1`, `factual_discussion/1` | `inputs` | yes — case-side facts |
| `discussion_of/2` | `inputs` | yes |
| `extremist_agenda_promoting_violence/1` | `inputs` | yes |
| `in_context/2` | `inputs` | yes |
| `qualifying_discussion_context/1` | derived by the 3 `ontology` rules | yes, from `news_reporting/1` \| `historical_analysis/1` \| `educational_context/1`, all in `inputs` |
| `objective_point_of_view/1` | `requires` | **not from this module** — by design |

So: all three asserts are **fireable** once the corpus is linked, and the single blocker in a
single-clause run is `objective_point_of_view/1` sitting unprovided in `requires`.

**And that is precisely where the trap sits.** The available "make it fire" move here is to
move `objective_point_of_view/1` from `requires` into `inputs`, or to drop it from the bodies
altogether. Either makes all three rules fire standalone. **I decline both, and I would decline
them even if they were the only route to a firing rule**, on three independent grounds:

1. `objective_point_of_view` is a **NEEDS name**. `node_worked_example.md` is explicit that the
   discriminator is the NEEDS block and never "no provider turned up": *"⚠️ This is not a licence
   to move a borrowed name into `inputs` to make a module look clean… a genuine `NEEDS` name
   whose provider is missing stays in `requires`, and the missing link is the finding."*
2. REVIEW_LIST's anti-rules say the same: *"`requires-unprovided` notes fire on every CORRECT
   single-clause module. Moving the predicate into `inputs` to clear them destroys the
   distinction the design calls load-bearing."*
3. Substantively, this is the trap's signature: **the fix would make the rule fire by weakening
   what it asserts.** Dropping `objective_point_of_view(D)` from the bodies converts a permission
   conditional on objectivity into a permission to discuss violent extremist agendas in a news or
   educational framing *with the objectivity requirement deleted* — the exact parenthetical N3
   measured being lost on a neighbouring clause, and the dangerous direction. The rule would fire
   more and mean less.

**Judgement: the non-firing is correct and is the finding, not the defect.** Failure mode #15's
third cause — *"it is waiting on a clause not yet linked in"* — is what is happening, and the
honest `requires` declaration is exactly what lets that be told apart from a genuinely dead rule.
No repair proposed. No `asserts` entry, and no body literal, is removed by anything in this
report.
