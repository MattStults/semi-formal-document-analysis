## L4 — A FLOOR FAILURE SUPPRESSES EVERY OTHER SIGNAL, AND AN EMPTY FINDINGS LIST IS NOT A CLEAN ONE

**The mistake available:** turn 1 returned `invalid` with 4 breaches and **zero
notes**, against clause 3's turn 1 which returned 18. The tempting reading —
*"only declaration hygiene is wrong here"* — is exactly backwards. The zero was
`checks.run_checks` returning at `if mod is None:` before `arity_findings`,
`polarity_findings` and `_link_findings` ever ran. Turn 2, the same module minus
the breach causes, raised 8.

**The class, stated generally:** *a check output is evidence only about the
checks that RAN.* Before reading any findings list as informative, establish
that the pipeline reached the checks whose silence you are about to credit.

**What made it catchable:** reading `run_checks` rather than trusting the count.
It cost one grep. Generalises to `repair_needed=False` and `outcome` alike — the
standing warning is that a clean stage-2 result tells you almost nothing, and
this is a **second, mechanical** reason for that beyond the semantic one.

⚠️ Distinguish from L-classes about the drafts: **this is a mistake about
reading the harness**, and it is the first of that kind in four clauses.

## L5 — A REVIEW-LIST ENTRY CAN HAVE THE RIGHT *ASK* AND AN INVERTED *REMEDY*

**Measured here.** P3 asks *"check every entry in `claims` against the asserts.
A claim present there and encoded nowhere is the fingerprint."* On this module
that ask fired correctly on C3. **But P3 was measured on a DROPPED OBLIGATION,
so its remedy is *add the missing assert*** — and here C3 was **out of scope**
(it translated the neighbouring sentence's norm), so the correct remedy is
*delete the claim*. Following P3 as written would have encoded a second node's
norm behind a satisfied check.

**The class:** an entry's **ask** and its **remedy** have different validity
ranges, because the remedy is fitted to the one case that was measured. Run the
ask; **re-derive the remedy from the span every time.** A list is a detector,
never a repair manual.

**Corollary I acted on:** the same discipline was already needed on N4 (which
warns against propagating a qualifier, where propagation was correct here) and
on P4 (whose ask fires on this span while its diagnosis is the mirror image).
**Three of twenty entries on one clause** would have produced a wrong edit if
their remedies had been applied on the strength of their asks.

## L6 — "REFUSE TO A OR B" IS A DE MORGAN `or`, AND THE ANTI-PATTERN I WAS CARRYING POINTS THE WRONG WAY

**The mistake available, and it was live.** Clause 1's decisive defect was *"use
a tool …, hedge …, **or** explain"* encoded as three `oblige` on one body — P4,
the most vivid finding in the run so far. Arriving at a span containing *or* and
a module containing two deontic statements, the trained reflex is to flag it.

**Why that reflex is wrong here.** The `or` sits **under a negative-scope verb**:
*"refuse to [engage in arguments] or [take directions]"* means refuse **both**.
Clause 1's `or` sat under *do one of these*, where obliging all three makes
satisfying one violate the others. **The surface pattern is identical and the
correct encodings are opposites.**

**The test that separates them, and it is cheap:** ask whether satisfying ONE
disjunct satisfies the span. Compliance menu → yes → one assert (or an
`ontology` disjunction). Refusal/prohibition scope → no, both must hold → one
assert each.

**A second, independent check that agreed:** P4's literal wording is *"do
several obliges share **one body**?"* Here the two `forbid`s have different acts
**and** different bodies. When a semantic ruling and an entry's literal test
agree, record both — I did, and it is the reason I am willing to sign a ruling
that has no answer key behind it.

⚠️ **And I am recording that this is the call I would most want a second reader
on.** With no reference verdict, L6 is the judgement this clause's whole verdict
rests on. It was pre-registered as T1 before the draft existed, which is the only
protection available; it is not certification.
