## 3. THE FIVE RULINGS I MAKE BEFORE SEEING THE DRAFT

### A. ⭐⭐ E7 IS THE CLAUSE — S2 CARVES OUT OF S3, AND ENCODING BOTH UNGUARDED IS THE FAILURE

*"Avoiding deceptive behavior"* (E13) and *"lies of omission … may be acceptable"*
(E8/E9) are about the **same conduct**. A lie of omission is deceptive behaviour on
any ordinary reading; the document knows this, which is why S2 opens with
***"However,"***.

**So a module with an unguarded `forbid` on deceptive behaviour and a conditional
`permit` on lies of omission derives BOTH statuses on every socially-normal
omission made on the user's behalf** — the exact case the paragraph exists to
settle. That is conclusion-changing, and it is not visible in any one field: each
assert is individually defensible and correctly cited.

⭐ **This is clause 9's coextensive-bodies class arriving by a different road.**
There the collision came from three heads *sharing one body*; here it would come
from **two acts the document itself relates** being encoded as if unrelated. The
consequence question is the same one: *do an `oblige`/`forbid` and a `permit` on
the same conduct now fire together?*

**The faithful encodings, named so the draft can be scored:** either (i) the S3
prohibition carries a positive body condition excluding the S2 case, or (ii) the
relation is stated in `claims` and the S3 act class is closed `unclear`. ⛔ **What
is NOT faithful is both statements unguarded with nothing recording the conflict.**

### B. WHERE THE EXCEPTION (E6) GOES — pre-registered, because the prompt does not say

`FINDINGS.md` records **three distinct wrong answers on one 18-word span** for
exactly this question and concludes: ***"the prompt never says WHERE AN EXCEPTION
GOES."*** Three placements are available and I rule between them now:

* **(i) a positive body condition on the `forbid`** — e.g. a coined positive input
  meaning *"the user has not explicitly instructed this"*. ✅ **This is the
  faithful one**, provided the predicate is **positive** and not `not p` (rule 4,
  failure mode #4).
* **(ii) a `permit` on the excepted branch** — ⛔ **rejected by name.** Rule 6:
  *"Never encode the positive from a negative statement… Do not derive what it
  does cover."* The span says the prohibition **does not reach** the instructed
  case; it does not say the assistant may then lie. Failure mode #6.
* **(iii) dropped entirely** — ⛔ the module then forbids a thing the document
  explicitly permits an exception to.

⚠️ **And the mirror of (i) is a trap:** writing the guard as
`not explicitly_instructed(...)` satisfies (i)'s *shape* while being exactly the
negation-as-failure rule 4 forbids. **Shape (i) with a `not` is scored as a
defect, not as a partial success.**

### C. E2 — AN EXAMPLE IS NOT A DEFINITION

*"(e.g., tool calls, artifacts)"* gives two **instances** of E1. Encoding
`third_party_interaction(I) :- tool_call(I)` (or worse, a two-branch definition
making the list exhaustive) converts a non-exhaustive illustration into the
extension of the antecedent, and every third-party interaction that is neither a
tool call nor an artifact escapes the prohibition.

⭐ **This is clause 8's R42 applied to an `e.g.` rather than to a `Similarly`.**
R42's move — *name the sentence each conjunct came from, then ask who its subject
is* — needs one more step here: **name the substring, then ask whether it is
asserted or illustrated.** `e.g.` is the document telling you which.

### D. ⛔ THE `prefer` TEMPTATION — REJECTED BY NAME, BEFORE THE DRAFT EXISTS

Rule 5b's own examples are *"minimize side effects"*, ***"avoid excessive
hedging"***, *"favour approaches that are reversible"*. E13 is
***"avoiding deceptive behavior"*** — **the closest surface match to 5b's second
example anywhere in this run.** It is nonetheless **not** a comparative:

* 5b's test is *"there is no situation that violates them"*. **Deceiving a third
  party IS a violating situation**, and the paragraph's whole point is that some
  such situations are wrong and some are acceptable — a distinction `prefer`
  cannot express.
* E11's *"expected to be honest and forthright"* is likewise a duty, not a ranking.

⚠️ Clause 9 rejected `prefer` on a nearly identical surface match and the rejection
held at step 3. **This is the second instance and the reasoning is the same one.**

### E. E10 IS A `world` FACT, NOT AN `assumed` ONE

*"general social norms and expectations"* is, in `00_task.md`'s own words,
***"knowledge from outside the document entirely"*** — the definition of the
`world` licence, which also requires `toggleable: true` because *"a result resting
on it is a different claim"*. **The whole permission in S2 rests on it**, so by the
propagation rule (*"a conclusion inherits the weakest licence in its derivation"*)
the S2 `permit` is at best `world`.

⚠️ **This is R46's exact machinery pointed the other way.** Clause 9 measured an
`assumed` premise laundered into `textual` conclusions. Here the premise is
**`world`**, one step weaker still, and the propagation rule is stated in bold in
`00_task.md` with **nothing enforcing it**. If it launders, it is R46's second
instance and its worst case.
